# TWIN SOLS: ECHO COUNCIL

## Five-minute pitch

### Slide 1 — Title and summary

Imagine you are ten years old and responsible for a Mars base. A solar warning arrives, power is limited, and you can protect either the oxygen recycler or the radiation shelter. Which one do you save?

Twin Sols: Echo Council puts children inside that decision. Across ten Martian days, the player leads a crew, manages the station, and learns that a successful mission plan always spends something.

### Slide 2 — Problem

The problem is not a lack of space facts. Children can learn that oxygen, food and shielding matter, yet still miss the logic that connects them.

Posters keep every system safely separate. Professional simulators place too much complexity on the screen. The challenge asks for the difficult middle: a child should understand why saving power tonight may reduce oxygen tomorrow, without needing an engineering degree.

### Slide 3 — Solution

Our answer is a ten-sol visual novel mission trainer. The player walks through Echo Station, talks with four specialists, and spends only three time blocks each day.

At the Council, every plan reveals what it gains, what it spends, and what might go wrong later. Our distinctive mechanic connects people to hardware. A repair may be technically correct, but an exhausted engineer can make it unsafe, and a crew member who has lost trust may refuse the risk.

### Slide 4 — How it works

The NASA data has a job inside the game. DONKI flare, solar energetic particle, and coronal mass ejection records select the training risk for Sol 2. OSDR research informs fatigue, recovery, health, and plant consequences. RadLab provides real ISS dose-rate units as a scale lesson. Mars Trek grounds rover routes in Gale Crater terrain.

We state the limits clearly: DONKI does not predict Mars conditions here, RadLab is not a Mars measurement, and Echo Station is fictional. If live data fails, the game labels cached or archived data instead of pretending it is current.

### Slide 5 — Demo

Here is one complete learning loop. A real solar-weather record raises the training risk. Theo explains what the data can and cannot tell us.

At the Council, the player chooses whether to reinforce the shelter, protect the rover science, or split power between both. The interface shows every resource before and after the choice. Then a consequence scene makes the result personal.

The player does not receive a green correct-answer badge. The debrief asks what the mission gained, what it spent, and what became riskier later.

### Slide 6 — Impact and next steps

Our learning target is simple: after one sol, a child should explain one trade-off in their own words.

The game runs in a browser with no account, and nine endings make replay part of the lesson. We have already tested every one of the 59,049 Council choice chains and confirmed that all nine endings remain reachable. That proves the simulation does not collapse into one winning path.

It does not prove learning yet, so our next step is honest child testing across ages seven to twelve, followed by bilingual support and a teacher debrief mode.

## Two-minute Q&A preparation

### How does NASA data change gameplay?

DONKI records select the Sol 2 training risk. The risk changes the radiation penalty and the pressure on the Council choice. OSDR themes affect fatigue, recovery, health and plants. RadLab supplies real ISS dose-rate units for comparison. Mars Trek grounds rover route decisions in Gale Crater terrain.

### Are you claiming DONKI predicts radiation on Mars?

No. The game calls it research training context. DONKI does not provide an operational Mars forecast in our prototype. RadLab values are also labelled as ISS comparisons, not Mars readings.

### What makes this educational rather than a space-themed story?

The story cannot progress without resource trade-offs. Every Council option changes a system now or creates a delayed consequence. The debrief asks the player to identify both the gain and the cost.

### What have you tested?

The automated mission test evaluates 59,049 Council choice chains and confirms that all nine endings are reachable. We have not claimed child learning results yet. That is our next validation step.

### How is the game accessible to children?

It runs in a browser without an account. It supports mouse, keyboard and touch, includes reduced-motion settings, explains technical words in context, and keeps three main resources visible.

### How did you use AI?

Codex supported implementation, debugging, UI iteration and presentation drafting. The team chose the concept, wrote the story direction, selected the mechanics and made the final project decisions.

### What would you build next?

We would test Sol 1 with ages 7–8, 9–10 and 11–12. Our target is that at least 80% finish without help and at least 80% explain one engineering trade-off. We would then add bilingual support and a teacher debrief mode.
